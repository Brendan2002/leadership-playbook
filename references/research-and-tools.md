## 1. Evidence checks

**How much do leaders matter? (Meindl, Ehrlich & Dukerich, 1985)** Observers tend to attribute organizational outcomes to leaders, especially when outcomes are extreme, and overestimate leaders' control. Credit and blame both inflate. The authors also note that believing your actions matter can be useful for someone in a leadership role, because it sustains effort where control is possible.
*Use:* after an unusually good or bad result, check conditions outside the team's control before crediting or blaming anyone. Treat leadership-book success stories as illustrations, not proof.

**Leadership literature vs reality (Pfeffer, 2016).** Much leadership writing prescribes what ought to be true (authenticity, trust, agreeableness) more than what evidence shows about getting things done. Behavior depends heavily on situation and role, and leadership skills — including building relationships and influence — can be learned rather than being fixed traits.
*Use:* weigh book prescriptions against what's actually happening; "I'm not that kind of person" isn't a reason to skip a needed behavior.

**Which behaviors matter most? (McKinsey, Feser, Mayol & Srinivasan, 2015).** A large survey across many organizations found that a small set of behaviors — effective problem solving, a strong results orientation, seeking different perspectives, and supporting others — distinguished organizations with strong leadership, especially at the front line. It's correlational and drawn from large organizations, and the authors note context matters.
*Use:* a short checklist for coaching a new lead, and it lines up with the books (structured problem solving, priorities and numbers, asking questions and inviting debate, recognition and support).

## 2. Leading without formal authority

**Leadership as collaborative practice (Raelin, 2014).** Leadership can be understood as something people do together, not only a property of the person in charge. Activities anyone can contribute:
- **Scanning**: finding information or resources that help.
- **Signaling**: drawing others' attention to an issue or effort.
- **Weaving**: building connections and shared understanding across groups.
- **Stabilizing**: feeding back on whether an effort is working.
- **Inviting** and **unleashing**: making sure everyone who wants to contribute can, especially quieter people.
- **Reflecting**: learning together from experience.

*Use:* when another department owns what you need, these describe what you can do — surface the issue, bring the right people together, report back on whether a fix holds — alongside a clear escalation.

**Influence.** Being useful to others, building relationships across groups, keeping commitments and using credible evidence make requests more persuasive. Use influence honestly.

## 3. Fairness and rolling out change

**Four kinds of fairness (Peus, 2012):**
- *Distributive*: do outcomes reflect contribution?
- *Procedural*: are the rules for deciding fair and understood, and do people get a voice?
- *Informational*: do people hear bad news as well as good, with enough predictability?
- *Interpersonal*: is everyone treated with respect — tough on the issue, respectful to the person?

When you can't give everyone what they want (shifts, assignments), procedural and informational fairness carry most of the weight.

**A persuasive change message covers:** why the current situation needs to change, why this change is the right one, that it can be done, that leadership supports it, and what's in it for the people affected. Be honest about downsides, and repeat the message more than once. Involving people early restores their sense of control.

**Resistance (Issah, 2018):** some resistance is rational — people weren't consulted or don't see why — and responds to involvement and explanation. Some is about status, recognition or fear of losing something, and needs a direct, respectful conversation. Self-awareness, self-regulation and empathy help a leader handle both.

## 4. Practical routines

**Weekly planning hour.** Once a week: look back (what didn't get done, what's unanswered, what caused friction and how to prevent it), look forward (the next one to three months — what needs to start now), choose priorities, block time for them first, leave buffer time for the unexpected, and color-code the calendar so you can see where time goes.

**Post-event or post-project review.** Review one recent piece of work with the people involved: what went right, what to improve, what was promised that someone must follow up on, and what other groups need to know. Then commit to **one** fix, not ten; teams can only carry out a few changes well at a time. When one problem keeps appearing, focus several reviews on it.

**Team communication.** Watch three things: how often people interact, how evenly participation is spread, and how much they connect with other teams. Respond quickly, recognize good work when you see it, draw quieter people in, and pair people who don't usually work together.

**Service standards.** Before asking people to "use judgment" with customers, write down what good looks like: show concern, listen and stay courteous, answer and explain, resolve fully the first time, and respond promptly. Then check whether it's happening.

**Decision quality check.** After a meeting, rate each decision on importance, acceptability to those affected, follow-up (is there a plan to implement and monitor it?) and quality of thinking. Weak follow-up is the most common gap.

## 5. Reflection questions

A four-way balance, after Koestenbaum's Leadership Diamond (vision, reality, ethics, courage), in paraphrase:
- **Reality:** What are the facts? What's in the way? How did I reach this conclusion?
- **Vision:** What am I trying to create? What would success look like a year from now?
- **Ethics:** Who is affected, and how? What's the decent thing to do here?
- **Courage:** What have I been avoiding doing or saying? What does success require of me?

Leaders are pulled between these (vision vs reality, care vs courage); the weakest one is often where growth pays off most. The courage questions are a good check before an escalation you've been putting off.

**Teamwork reminders:** fix the system (scheduling, training, handoffs) before blaming individuals; assume a group is less of a team than it looks until shown otherwise; teach and learn leadership in every direction — yourself, your team, your peers and upward.

## 6. Your own development

A useful development plan:
- Focuses on one to three areas with the biggest impact.
- Uses specific, measurable goals tied to the team's and organization's needs.
- Builds on at least one real strength, not only gaps.
- Leans on experience and relationships more than courses. (The common "70-20-10" split is a rule of thumb with limited evidence.)
- Gets revisited quarterly, with support from others, feedback from colleagues, and measures of progress.
