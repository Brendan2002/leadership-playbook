# Good to Great (Jim Collins, 2001): frameworks for a team lead

A paraphrased guide to the book's main ideas and how a team lead without full authority can apply them. Read the book for the full research and examples.

## 1. What the study claims

Collins's team looked for companies that performed at or below the stock market for about fifteen years and then beat it substantially for the next fifteen, and compared them with similar companies that didn't make the jump. The central argument: being "good enough" removes the pressure to question assumptions and drop familiar activities, which is what keeps organizations from becoming excellent.

Some things the researchers expected to matter didn't distinguish the companies: celebrity outside CEOs, executive pay structure, technology itself, mergers, change-management programs, or being in an attractive industry.

The findings are organized as disciplined people → disciplined thought → disciplined action, with the flywheel describing how they build momentum.

**What "great" means here:** sustained financial returns under specific criteria — not best employer, most ethical, or most socially valuable.

## 2. Level 5 leadership

Lasting results came from leaders who combined personal humility with intense professional will: modest about themselves, uncompromising about results, and ambitious for the organization rather than their own reputation. Collins describes a five-level hierarchy (capable individual → team contributor → competent manager → effective leader → Level 5) as cumulative capabilities.

Two habits stand out:
- **Window and mirror:** credit others and circumstances when things go well; look at your own decisions when they don't.
- **Building successors:** the organization should get stronger, not weaker, when the leader is absent.

**For a team lead:**
- Credit the team specifically for good execution.
- When something goes wrong, check whether expectations, training, resources and decisions were clear; separate your responsibility from decisions others own.
- Test: does the team become more capable because of your leadership, including when you aren't there?

## 3. First who, then what

Get capable people into the right roles before locking in a detailed plan; good people adapt when plans change. A team that depends on one person's thinking is fragile.

Practical disciplines:
1. **When in doubt, don't hire** just to relieve pressure; keep looking.
2. **When a people change is needed, act** — but first check whether the issue is the person or the placement, and try a different role.
3. **Put strong people on the biggest opportunities**, not permanently on the worst recurring problems.

Rigorous, not ruthless: clear expectations and thoughtful decisions, not constant restructuring. Growth is limited by how many capable people you can bring in.

**For a team lead:**
- Plan workload to actual staffing capability; a bigger calendar can weaken the team if it means untrained combinations or constant personal coverage.
- Evaluate follow-through, ability to learn, fit with the responsibility, real availability, and the support someone needs — not just enthusiasm.
- Hiring and firing usually aren't the lead's decision; apply this to assignments, training and recommendations.

## 4. Confront the brutal facts

Good decisions start with facing reality. Leaders undermine this when people learn which answers the leader wants and filter what they report. False hope that later collapses demotivates more than honest news.

Four practices:
1. Lead with questions before answers.
2. Encourage real debate.
3. Review failures without blame.
4. Build **red-flag mechanisms**: rules that force attention or action, not just reports. (A schedule showing an open shift is information; a rule that requires escalation if coverage isn't confirmed by a set day makes it actionable.)

**The Stockdale Paradox:** keep unwavering confidence that you'll prevail while confronting the hardest facts of your current situation. "The program can succeed" and "this week is at risk" can both be true.

## 5. The Hedgehog Concept

Focus comes from understanding the overlap of three questions:
1. What can you be the best at (and what can't you)?
2. What drives your economic engine — one measure of "value per unit of something"?
3. What are people deeply passionate about?

Competence isn't the same as being best. Growth for its own sake is a warning sign. Clarity usually takes years of questions, debate, decisions and reviews — an iterative process, often run by a small standing group that debates to understand rather than to win.

**For a team lead:** you won't set company strategy, but you can ask these questions about your team's work: which activities you do especially well, what value each creates, and which ones consume resources without clear return. Agree on what each kind of activity is *for* before choosing how to measure it.

## 6. A culture of discipline

Bureaucracy grows to compensate for unclear expectations and the wrong people in roles. The alternative is freedom and responsibility within a clear framework: consistent standards, room for judgment, ownership of outcomes. A disciplined culture works without the leader policing it.

**Stop-doing lists** matter as much as to-do lists; budgets should decide what to fully fund and what to drop.

**For a team lead:** show people what good looks like before saying "use your judgment," and keep your own stop-doing list (duplicate tracking, manual reminders, tasks a trained team member could own).

## 7. Technology as an accelerator

Technology accelerated momentum that already existed; it rarely created it. Adopt tools that clearly fit what you're trying to do, not because others have them.

Before adding a tool: what decision gets better, what repeated work gets easier, what information becomes more reliable, and how will you know it helped? No tool creates authority, staffing capacity or agreement on an unclear process.

## 8. The flywheel and the doom loop

Results build like pushing a heavy flywheel: many consistent pushes, no single breakthrough moment. The **doom loop** is the opposite: disappointing results → dramatic new initiative → change of direction → disappointment → restart.

**For a team lead:** an illustrative flywheel is better recruiting → better training → more reliable execution → better results and relationships → better opportunities → a stronger team. Adding volume while the same staffing and training weaknesses persist breaks it. Before scrapping a system, ask whether it's a doom-loop reset or a genuine stop-doing decision.

## 9. From good to great to lasting

Collins treats this book as a prequel to *Built to Last*: sustained results, then core values and a purpose beyond money, preserved while practices change.

## 10. Caveats

- Historical and observational; association doesn't establish cause.
- "Great" is a financial definition.
- Most examples are CEO-level; translate to what a team lead controls, and recommend upward for the rest.
- Use it to sharpen questions, not as a formula.
