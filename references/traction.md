# Traction (Gino Wickman): frameworks for a team lead

A paraphrased guide to the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS) and how a team lead can adapt pieces of it. Read the book for the full tools and templates.

**Scope:** EOS is designed for small-to-midsize companies with a real leadership team. Full adoption is a company decision; a team lead can borrow the pieces below for their own area.

## 1. The six components

Organizations stall when they lack a shared operating system — people work hard but with different priorities, unclear ownership, inconsistent processes, weak measures, unproductive meetings, and issues that never get solved. EOS addresses six areas: **Vision** (shared direction), **People** (right people in right roles), **Data** (a few numbers watched weekly), **Issues** (solving real problems), **Process** (consistent core work), and **Traction** (priorities turning into done work). Wickman includes a self-assessment to score how strong each area is.

## 2. Letting go of control

Leaders often hold decisions and knowledge centrally even when it limits growth. Breaking through requires the ability to **simplify, delegate and elevate, predict, systemize, and structure**.

**For a team lead:** routine work should be able to run through trained people, clear instructions and a defined escalation path. Delegation needs an outcome, authority, resources and follow-up; handing off a task without them just moves the confusion.

## 3. Vision

A short planning document answers: core values; core focus (purpose and niche); a long-range target; marketing strategy (target customer, what makes you different, your proven process, any guarantee); a three-year picture; a one-year plan; quarterly priorities; and an issues list. Share the vision repeatedly — once is never enough.

**For a team lead:** clarify what your team's work is for within the company's direction (revenue, trial, awareness, relationships, leads). Without agreement, people judge the same work by different standards. Propose options; company values and strategy stay with leadership.

## 4. People

Two separate questions: does this person fit the organization's values, and do they fit the role? Someone well-liked can be in the wrong role; someone skilled can damage the team through behavior.

- **Values check:** rate people against stated values based on observed behavior.
- **Role fit:** does the person *understand* the role, *want* it, and have the *capacity* (ability and time) for it? All three are needed.
- **Accountability chart:** define functions and responsibilities first, then put names in them. One person can hold two seats; two people can't share one.

**For a team lead:** for an assignment, check whether the person understands the full job, genuinely accepts it, can actually do and get to it, and has had the training and clearance it needs. Make boundaries explicit between functions you coordinate and those others own (HR, payroll, approvals, resources).

## 5. Data

A **scorecard** of roughly 5–15 weekly numbers, each with a definition, one owner, a target and a visible trend. Favor leading indicators you can act on over results that arrive too late. Getting the numbers right takes a few months. Eventually everyone has a number tied to their role.

**Candidate measures** for an operations-heavy team (illustrative, not targets): upcoming shifts or tasks without confirmed coverage; upcoming work missing readiness items; people able to lead independently; overdue follow-ups; qualified opportunities generated; contribution after direct costs; the lead's own hours spent filling routine gaps.

**Qualification:** measure what people can reasonably influence and read numbers in context. A number starts an investigation; it doesn't replace judgment. Set a baseline before ambitious targets.

## 6. Issues

Use **Identify, Discuss, Solve**:
- **Identify** the real issue, which is often a layer or two beneath the symptom ("people keep asking questions" may mean unclear instructions).
- **Discuss** it briefly and stay on topic.
- **Solve** it with a decision, an owner and an action; then check that it worked.

Work the most important few issues first, not the list in order. Deciding matters more than deciding perfectly.

**Escalation format:** what's happening, the evidence, why it matters, the decision needed, your proposed next step, and who should own the decision.

## 7. Process

Most companies have a handful of core processes. Name them consistently, document the critical steps at a high level (not every detail), package them where people actually use them, and make sure leaders follow them first. Question "we've always done it that way," and don't buy technology for its own sake.

**For a team lead:** for each core process (recruiting, onboarding, training, scheduling, preparation, execution, closeout, follow-up), ask what starts it, who owns it, what information is required, what must happen, what confirms completion, and where it goes next. Handoffs between people are where most failures hide.

## 8. Traction: priorities and meetings

**Quarterly priorities ("Rocks"):** a few 90-day priorities, each specific and measurable with one owner. Keep the list short; park new ideas until next quarter. Aim to complete most, not all.

**A priority vs a to-do:** a priority is a quarterly outcome; a to-do is a one-week commitment toward it. "Improve staffing" is too vague; describe what will exist or work differently.

**Weekly meeting:** same day, time and agenda every week, starting and ending on time. Brief reviews of good news, numbers, priority status, headlines and last week's to-dos, then most of the time on solving issues, then a recap of actions and a rating. Off-track items go to the issues list instead of being discussed during the reviews.

**Quarterly and annual sessions** review progress, reset priorities and revisit the plan.

## 9. Implementation order

Wickman recommends starting with ownership and execution (accountability chart, priorities, the weekly meeting, the scorecard) before the full vision document and process documentation, and mastering the tools in leadership before rolling them out.

## 10. Adapting it as a team lead

Most adaptable on your own: clear ownership, a small scorecard, Identify–Discuss–Solve, process handoffs, and a short weekly review (numbers, priorities, to-dos, then issues) sized to your team and authority. Evidence for EOS comes mainly from implementation experience and client stories.
